# One Ordinary Day: source ledger

Curated 8 November 2026. Ten dated diary accounts. The HTML is self-contained and uses editorial paraphrases with short identifying excerpts. Each documented moment points to the same dated entry as its story. No fictional dialogue, reconstructed clock times, invented possessions or invented motives are used.

The scene is an original symbolic WebGL still life, not a historical reconstruction. Quotes are short and used for identification and commentary. Source transcriptions and images are not bundled in full.

## Coverage and limits

English-language Britain and North America only, 1660-1941. Literacy, survival, publication and editorial selection shape the set. Several diarists were privileged or later became famous. Ordinary activities are the subject, not a claim that the writers were socially typical. Documented means stated in the cited source, not independently corroborated. Explicit record gaps are part of each story.

## Charlotte Forten | 24 May 1854

**Edition and context:** National Humanities Center, selections from the journal edited by Ray Allen Billington (1953), entry of 24 May 1854.

**Primary entry:** https://nationalhumanitiescenter.org/pds/maai/identity/text3/charlottefortenjournal.pdf

**Use:** Before the sun gets ahead. Arithmetic, sewing, the water, and a new Dickens story.

**Limits:** The wake-up time is explicit. The later clock times, meals, route and bedtime are not. A diary is her account, not an independently verified recording.

**Context:** Forten came from a prominent free Black family. The edition places her in Salem after exclusion from white schools in Philadelphia. Her access to education was hard-won, not something every young woman could assume.

## Thomas Turner | 25 February 1754

**Edition and context:** Thomas Turner diary site, entry of 25 February 1754, based on Dean K. Worcester's 1948 transcription. Edition notes: https://thomasturner.org.uk/about

**Primary entry:** https://thomasturner.org.uk/

**Use:** Tea after a rumour. A borrowed horse, an unwanted report, and the relief of coming home.

**Limits:** The visit and trip are in narrative order, without clock times. Only the return is placed in the evening. Pleasure is Turner's reported assessment, not his wife's testimony.

**Context:** Turner was both a village shopkeeper and a parish officer. This is a published transcription of a diary, not a complete record of the household. The site explains that its underlying Worcester transcription is selective and not fully accurate.

## Mary Cooper | 1 August 1769

**Edition and context:** National Humanities Center, excerpts from The Diary of Mary Cooper, edited by Field Horne (1981), entry of 1 August 1769; context from the edition's introduction and notes.

**Primary entry:** https://nationalhumanitiescenter.org/pds/becomingamer/peoples/text5/marycooper.pdf

**Use:** Two dogs in the bedroom. Finished work, a short visit, and guests who will not leave her in peace.

**Limits:** Work tasks and exact hours are not listed. Anxiety, relief and irritation are hers as recorded; the guests' motives and the experiences of others in the household are not supplied.

**Context:** Cooper's household farmed near Oyster Bay and also accommodated travellers. The edition identifies enslaved people in her household elsewhere in the diary. Her account of hard work must not erase that unequal setting or turn her into a representative of everyone on the farm.

## Martha Ballard | 10 October 1785

**Edition and context:** DoHistory, transcription of Martha Ballard's diary, page covering 8-17 October 1785; selected entry: 10 October. Background: https://www.dohistory.org/diary/about.html Place context: https://dohistory.org/timeline/timeline3.html

**Primary entry:** https://www.dohistory.org/diary/1785/10/17851008_txt.html

**Use:** Two houses to visit. A morning call, another after noon, and a household network at work.

**Limits:** No clock times, travel method, treatment or payment is recorded for this date. The statement that the Westons were better is Ballard's observation, not a modern medical assessment.

**Context:** Ballard's diary recorded her healing and midwifery alongside domestic and community life. Its compressed names and spelling require caution. We do not turn an ambiguous surname or an unnamed group into a more detailed cast of characters.

## Dorothy Wordsworth | 15 April 1802

**Edition and context:** The Grasmere Journal, entry of 15 April 1802, online transcription. Date and setting also checked against Lancaster University's FutureLearn reading: https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/william-wordsworth/0/steps/9880

**Primary entry:** https://wordsworth-coleridge.com/home/dorothy/daffodils

**Use:** A walk in the wind. Dinner first. Then shelter, rough water, and flowers along the shore.

**Limits:** The source says after dinner, without a clock time. The online passage used here does not give the whole evening, a precise route duration or a bedtime.

**Context:** Wordsworth later became celebrated for these journals. She and her brother had the time and connections for this visit and walk; their day is not a sample of everyone's working life. The source is a personal observation, not a botanical survey.

## Anne Lister | 27 August 1817

**Edition and context:** West Yorkshire Archive Service, SH:7/ML/E/1/0034, entry headed Wednesday 27 August 1817. The page contains several dates; this story uses only the 27th.

**Primary entry:** https://www.catalogue.wyjs.org.uk/Record.aspx?id=CC00001%2F7%2F9%2F6%2F1%2F34&src=CalmView.Catalog

**Use:** A wheel, a letter, a lecture. Study in the morning; engineering and company later on.

**Limits:** The entry explicitly gives four, seven and near eleven in its narrative. We leave its compressed waking and retiring notation uninterpreted. The desk and apparatus shown here are symbolic, not reconstructions.

**Context:** Lister was from a landowning family, not an economically typical worker. The archive transcription includes difficult shorthand, marginal times and encoded personal passages. Here we use only clearly readable activities in the dated entry, without guessing what ambiguous time notation means.

## Emilie Davis | 1 January 1863

**Edition and context:** Kaye Wise Whitehead, Reconstructing the Life of a Colored Woman: The Pocket Diaries of Emilie F. Davis, Pennsylvania Magazine of History and Biography (2011), p. 561; reproduces the diary entry of 1 January 1863. Historical context: https://www.archives.gov/exhibits/featured-documents/emancipation-proclamation

**Primary entry:** https://journals.psu.edu/pmhb/article/download/60404/60363/0

**Use:** A party after the jubilee. A few lines hold a public change and a private evening.

**Limits:** Only a short quoted entry is available in this source. It does not establish her waking time, a particular church she personally attended that day, the party's start time or its guest list.

**Context:** Davis was a free Black woman whose work included sewing and domestic service. The Proclamation did not free every enslaved person everywhere in the United States: it applied to designated rebellious areas and excluded loyal border states. Its reach is checked against the National Archives, rather than the journal article's over-broad explanatory sentence.

## Ebenezer Parkman | 8 January 1776

**Edition and context:** Colonial Society of Massachusetts, Ebenezer Parkman's World, diary for 1776, entry of 8 January. Background: https://www.colonialsociety.org/documents/ebenezer-parkman-world/introduction

**Primary entry:** https://www.colonialsociety.org/documents/ebenezer-parkman-world-diary-1776

**Use:** Still no dinner. House calls, food at last, and a letter in the evening.

**Limits:** Most clock times are absent. We can follow the order of visits and the evening letter, but not assign a timetable, diagnose the patients, or know the conversations not written down.

**Context:** Parkman was an educated Congregational minister whose diary also records household resources and local obligations. His viewpoint is not that of every person he visits. In particular, the sick neighbours do not speak for themselves in this entry.

## Samuel Pepys | 2 January 1659/60

**Edition and context:** The Diary of Samuel Pepys, online entry for 2 January 1659/60. Uses the diary text above the modern reader comments; those comments are not evidence for these beats.

**Primary entry:** https://www.pepysdiary.com/diary/1660/01/02/

**Use:** Bread and cheese will do. Failed appointments, borrowed money, cards, singing, and a late bite.

**Limits:** Nine o'clock is explicit; most other hours are not. Rumours Pepys hears in Westminster Hall are not treated as verified political news. We do not give his wife's illness a modern diagnosis.

**Context:** Pepys was connected to a powerful patron and later became a senior naval administrator. His movements are not a typical poor Londoner's day. The source dates this entry 1659/60 under the old English year convention; this display preserves that label rather than silently converting calendars.

## Nella Last | 15 January 1941

**Edition and context:** BBC History, War Diary of Nella Last, private-life extracts, dated 15 January 1941. Published 1 July 2001. Based on Last's Mass Observation diary.

**Primary entry:** https://www.bbc.co.uk/history/british/britain_wwtwo/nella_last_01.shtml

**Use:** Who works for a living?. A generous lunch becomes a disagreement about the value of housework.

**Limits:** The train is a plan in the entry, not proof of the exact departure. The BBC selection does not document her whole morning or evening. Dialogue is paraphrased here rather than recreated as a scene.

**Context:** Last wrote for Mass Observation, a project recording everyday British life. These are brief excerpts published by the BBC, not the entire diary for this date. Wartime separation is part of the setting, but this story centres the household exchange she records.

## Editorial checks

- All ten people and dates tied to entries read in the source editions.
- Every documented beat includes a short source excerpt.
- Every gap has no purported quotation.
- All 55 moments reviewed against source text; 44 documented moments and 11 explicit gaps.
- No weekday labels are needed; Pepys's source dating convention is preserved.
- Diary voices are not neutral witnesses: rumour remains rumour, observation remains attributed, and other people's minds are not invented.
- Cooper's enslaving household context is not omitted.
- The reach of the Emancipation Proclamation is checked against the National Archives, not the journal article's inaccurate summary.
- Turner's edition notes warn that its source transcription is selective and not fully accurate.
- Last's account is presented as a brief BBC selection, not a full daily reconstruction.
- Source addresses are selectable in the gallery because its sandbox intentionally blocks popups. No shared sandbox permissions are weakened.
