title: "Stepping: the reflex that pretends to be walking"

Hold a newborn upright with the feet touching a flat surface and something surprising happens: the legs make coordinated, alternating stepping movements, as if the baby were already walking. This newborn stepping appears within days of birth and looks like a preview of a skill that will not truly arrive for about a year.

Then it vanishes. By around two months the stepping response fades, and for decades textbooks explained this as the brain suppressing a primitive reflex until the cortex matured. Developmental psychologist Esther Thelen showed the story was incomplete: as babies grow, their legs gain weight faster than strength, so the movement disappears partly for mechanical reasons. Newborns held waist-deep in water, which buoys the legs, keep stepping.

Stepping is a perfect first example of this course's theme: development is not a program simply switching on. It is the body, brain and environment negotiating in real time, an idea lesson 3 explores in depth ([Thelen & Fisher, 1982](https://doi.org/10.1037//0012-1649.18.5.760)).
