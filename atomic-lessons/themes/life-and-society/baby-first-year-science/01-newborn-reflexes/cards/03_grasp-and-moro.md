Press a finger into a newborn's palm and the fingers clamp shut automatically. This palmar grasp can be surprisingly strong, though it is a reflex, not controlled strength, and you should never test how much weight it holds. Its partner in the feet, the plantar grasp, curls the toes when the sole is pressed.

The Moro reflex is the famous one. When a baby feels a sudden loss of support or hears a loud noise, the arms fly outward and open, then sweep back in toward the body, often with crying. Parents sometimes mistake it for fear; it is an automatic startle pattern, present from birth and normally fading between about two and six months.

What matters scientifically is the pattern, not the drama. Clinicians look for responses that are present, roughly symmetric on both sides, and that fade on schedule, because reflexes report on specific neural circuits. A markedly one-sided or absent response is a standard reason for a professional to look more closely.
