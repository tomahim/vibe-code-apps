Hearing the ball while a ball rolls by seems straightforward, but consider the puzzle: the word could mean the object, its color, its rolling, its material, or this moment. Philosopher Willard Quine called this the indeterminacy of reference. Yet children guess right most of the time - how?

Research points to layered cues. Children apply default assumptions: a new word for an unfamiliar object tends to name the whole object, not its parts; a second word for a familiar object must mean something else - a part, a property, a substance. Adults supply social cues: eye gaze, pointing, and naming whatever the child is already attending to, which lesson 7 showed is the most learnable moment.

Grammar helps too, in both directions. By the second year, children exploit sentence frames - this is a sib versus this is sib - to guess whether a word names a thing or a property. Word learning is detective work with many informants.
