title: "Fast mapping: one exposure, one word"

Around 18 months, word learning accelerates into what is often called the vocabulary explosion, with children adding new words daily. The mechanism behind it was demonstrated in a classic 1978 experiment by Susan Carey and Elsa Bartlett. A preschool child was casually asked to bring the tray that is chromium, not the red one - chromium being an unfamiliar color word. From that single incidental exposure, children formed a partial, usable mapping: chromium is some color, different from red.

This is fast mapping: sketching a word's meaning from one encounter and refining it with later evidence. It explains how children can absorb dozens of words a week without formal teaching - each book, bath and breakfast supplies trials.

Fast mapping is an inference engine, not a memorization trick. Children use the words they already know, the speaker's gaze and gesture, and the situation to guess what a new word must mean - and the guesses, though imperfect, stick well enough to be tuned over time ([Carey & Bartlett, 1978](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/418001/)).
