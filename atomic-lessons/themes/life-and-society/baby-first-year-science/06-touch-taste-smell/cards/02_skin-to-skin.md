The strongest evidence for touch's importance comes from skin-to-skin care: a newborn placed upright, chest to chest, against a caregiver's bare skin. Studies of this practice, especially in the first hours after birth, associate it with more stable heart rate, breathing and temperature, less crying, and higher rates of breastfeeding initiation.

The mechanism is regulation. A newborn's systems for controlling temperature, heart rhythm and stress are immature, and close body contact supplies external regulation while the internal systems develop. Kangaroo care, the extended form of skin-to-skin, showed such clear benefits for preterm and low-birth-weight infants that the WHO recommends it as standard care.

This reframes cuddling as physiology. Holding a baby is not merely affection - though it is that too; it is an external support system for a body still learning to run itself ([WHO kangaroo care](https://www.who.int/activities/caring-for-newborns-in-the-first-days-of-life)).
