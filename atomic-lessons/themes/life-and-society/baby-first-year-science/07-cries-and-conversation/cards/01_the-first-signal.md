title: "The first signal: crying"

Crying is a newborn's only broadcast channel, and it is engineered to be impossible to ignore. Adult brains respond to infant cries with speed and urgency that few other sounds provoke - parents and non-parents alike show rapid attention capture, which is precisely the point. A creature that cannot move, feed or warm itself needs a signal that mobilizes others.

Crying also changes over the first months in a pattern so regular it has a name: the normal crying curve. Across many cultures and even in other mammals, crying increases from birth, peaks around six to eight weeks, and declines toward three to four months. The peak is a feature of development, not a parenting failure.

Early on, cries are graded signals - intensity carries meaning more than type. The popular idea that parents can reliably distinguish a hunger cry from a pain cry has weak scientific support; context and intensity are far more informative than acoustic categories.
