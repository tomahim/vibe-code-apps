The Harvard Center on the Developing Child popularized a tennis metaphor for these exchanges: serve and return. The baby serves - a coo, a look, a gesture, later a babble or a word - and the caregiver returns with a response: eye contact, a name, a smile, a sound of their own. Thousands of these micro-rallies accumulate over the first year.

The claim goes beyond warmth. Responsive back-and-forth is proposed as a primary builder of brain architecture, language and self-regulation; its absence - chronic unresponsiveness - is a documented risk factor in studies of deprivation. The strongest version of the evidence is causal where it can be: interventions that increase caregiver responsiveness improve child outcomes.

Serve and return also hands the baby some control. When adults follow the baby's focus - naming what the baby is already looking at - learning is faster than when adults redirect attention. The baby serves; the skill of the adult is noticing what was served ([Harvard serve-and-return](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/key-concept/serve-and-return/)).
