title: "Tuning in: the first year of speech perception"
archived: false

Young infants begin with a remarkable ability: they can distinguish sound contrasts used in any human language, including ones their own language does not use. Patricia Kuhl's research showed that by about six months, experience with the native language begins reshaping this ability - perception of native vowel contrasts sharpens - and over the following months, sensitivity to non-native contrasts declines, a process called perceptual narrowing.

This is learning in its purest form: the brain specializes for the sound system it actually hears. A Japanese-learning infant gradually stops distinguishing r from l, while an English-learning infant sharpens that very contrast.

Perceptual narrowing is not a loss to mourn; it is the brain betting on its environment, trading universal sensitivity for expert precision. The timing of this tuning, mostly across the second half of the first year, is one argument for why early language exposure matters so much ([Kuhl et al., Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1736364)).
