title: "From startle to search: locating sounds"

In the first days, babies respond to sound globally: a sudden noise triggers a startle, a cry, or a pause in sucking. Localizing a sound - turning precisely toward its source - develops over the first months as the brain learns to compare the timing and loudness differences between the two ears.

By about four to six months, most babies turn reliably toward an interesting sound or a familiar voice, a skill pediatricians watch for. This turn-toward-voice behavior is more than a milestone: it is the auditory system linking sound to the social world, and it enables the face-to-face exchanges that feed language.

Newborn hearing screening, now standard in many countries, exploits objective measures like otoacoustic emissions - faint echoes the healthy inner ear produces - so hearing loss can be identified in the first days of life, long before behavioral signs would be reliable. Early detection changes outcomes because the language-learning window opens so early.
