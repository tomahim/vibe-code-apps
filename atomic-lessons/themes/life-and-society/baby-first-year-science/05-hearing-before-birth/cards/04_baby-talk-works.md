title: "Why baby talk works"

Adults across cultures instinctively shift their speech with babies: higher pitch, wider pitch swings, slower tempo, exaggerated vowels, simpler phrases. This register is called infant-directed speech, and babies overwhelmingly prefer to listen to it. In Anne Fernald's classic experiments, infants turned more often toward recordings of infant-directed than adult-directed speech.

The preference is functional. The exaggerated melody carries emotional information a preverbal baby can use - approval, warning, comfort - and the stretched, clearer vowels make speech sounds easier to tell apart. Infant-directed speech is not babying the baby; it is speech acoustically adapted to a beginner's auditory system.

Even the repetitiveness serves learning. Hearing the same words in the same routines - bath time, diaper changes, goodnight - lets the baby bind sound patterns to recurring events, which is the raw material of first comprehension.
