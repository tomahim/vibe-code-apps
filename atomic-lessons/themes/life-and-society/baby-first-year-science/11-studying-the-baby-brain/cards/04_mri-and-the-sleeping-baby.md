title: "MRI: anatomy from sleeping babies"

MRI delivers the detailed pictures: cortical folding, white-matter tracts, myelination, growth curves. The catch is motion: an MRI scan demands stillness for minutes at a time inside a loud tube. Infant MRI therefore traditionally meant scanning during natural sleep at night, with careful sound dampening - an entire subfield of baby-wrangling logistics - and historically, sedation, which researchers now avoid for research.

From such scans came the maps of the first-year growth surge: rapid cortical expansion, the orderly march of myelination from sensory to association areas, and individual growth trajectories now tracked by large projects like the Developing Human Connectome Project, which scanned babies from before term through infancy.

Awake infant fMRI - measuring brain responses to stimuli - exists but remains heroic: short scans, movie-watching toddlers, heavy preprocessing. Most functional claims about the awake infant brain still rest on EEG and fNIRS, with MRI anchoring the anatomy underneath.
