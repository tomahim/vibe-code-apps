If skills are assembled rather than switched on, what does thousands of repetitions a day actually construct? The modern answer is perception-action coupling: the baby learns to read the world in terms of what their body can currently do. A slope is not an angle; it is climbable or risky given today's strength and balance.

With practice, babies also learn to vary forces and timing: how hard to push, how fast to move, when to freeze. Adolph's falling data suggests the calibration includes learning what losing balance feels like and how to catch it, which is why new walkers improve fastest where they wobble most.

This is a satisfying place to end the movement subtheme. Reflexes supply raw material, the motor cascade supplies the sequence, and practice supplies the mechanism. The same pattern - built-in beginnings shaped by experience - will reappear when we turn to the senses.
