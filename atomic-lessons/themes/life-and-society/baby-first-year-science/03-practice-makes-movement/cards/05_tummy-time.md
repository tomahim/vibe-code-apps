The most famous practical application of the practice principle is tummy time: supervised periods when an awake baby plays lying on their stomach. From that position, lifting the head, pushing up on the arms and eventually pivoting are workouts for exactly the muscles and balance systems that sitting, crawling and walking will need.

The practice question became urgent in the 1990s, when the highly successful Back to Sleep campaign cut infant deaths by putting babies to sleep on their backs - and babies suddenly spent far less time on their stomachs. Pediatric guidance responded by adding supervised awake tummy time from the early weeks, starting with short bouts of a minute or two, several times a day.

Studies link regular awake prone time with earlier attainment of prone-related milestones like rolling and crawling. The effects are modest, not magic, and babies vary in how much they enjoy it. The evidence supports frequent short, playful sessions, with the safety boundary clear: tummy time is for awake, watched play; sleep happens on the back ([AAP guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/english/ages-stages/baby/pages/tummy-time-how-to-help-your-baby-get-strong.aspx)).
