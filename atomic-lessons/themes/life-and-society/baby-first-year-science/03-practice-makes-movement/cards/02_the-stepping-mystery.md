title: "The stepping mystery"

The case that cracked the maturation story was newborn stepping. As lesson 1 described, this early walking-like movement fades around two months. The classic explanation was neural: the maturing cortex suppresses a primitive reflex until walking begins.

Esther Thelen tested that explanation in the 1980s and found it wanting. She noticed that as babies grow, their legs gain fat - and therefore weight - faster than they gain muscle strength. When she held newborns waist-deep in water, buoying their legs, stepping continued past the age it normally vanishes. Adding small weights to younger babies' ankles made stepping stop earlier. Same nerves, different mechanics.

Even more striking, giving babies a few minutes of daily treadmill practice kept the stepping pattern alive and led to earlier independent walking. A skill the old theory said was switched off by the brain could be preserved by exercise. Development, Thelen argued, emerges from the whole system, not from a genetic clock alone ([Thelen & Fisher, 1982](https://doi.org/10.1037//0012-1649.18.5.760)).
