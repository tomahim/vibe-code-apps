title: "The old story: development as a program"
archived: false
---
For much of the twentieth century, motor development was explained as maturation. Arnold Gesell and his contemporaries documented the orderly sequence of skills - head control, sitting, crawling, walking - and concluded that the nervous system simply ripens on a fixed timetable, the way a flower opens. Practice and experience were considered almost irrelevant decorations on a genetic program.

This view left a useful legacy: careful norms describing what babies typically do at each age, ancestors of today's milestone lists. But as a mechanism it explains little. If maturation alone drove development, all healthy babies would walk within weeks of each other, and nothing caregivers did would shift the schedule.

Neither is true. The WHO windows span nearly a year for walking, and experiments show that daily practice changes when skills appear. The maturation story described the pattern but missed the process - and the process turns out to be far more interesting.
