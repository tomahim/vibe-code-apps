Karen Adolph spent decades measuring what practice actually looks like. In her lab, new crawlers and new walkers judged whether they could descend slopes of varying steepness. Experienced crawlers made accurate decisions; when the same babies later began walking, their hard-won judgment did not transfer, and they misjudged slopes all over again. Each posture, from crawling to cruising to walking, demands its own calibration.

The scale of practice is the other finding. Adolph's measurements show new walkers take thousands of steps and fall dozens of times per day, across varied surfaces and obstacles. Spread over weeks, that is an extraordinary volume of tailored feedback, far more than any formal training could design.

Her summary is memorable: babies do not just learn to move, they learn to learn to move. What accumulates is not a fixed skill but a flexible ability to read each new situation - a point that generalizes well beyond walking.
