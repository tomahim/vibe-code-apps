Does babbling require hearing? Laura-Ann Petitto's research with deaf infants gave a decisive answer in two parts. Deaf babies exposed only to spoken language show sharply reduced and delayed vocal babbling - audible feedback from one's own voice and from caregivers is necessary for normal vocal babble to take off.

But deaf babies exposed to sign language babble with their hands: they produce repetitive, syllable-like manual movements - hand babbling - with the same timing and structure as vocal babble. The drive to babble is therefore not about the mouth; it is about the language system rehearsing its units in whatever channel is available.

This is among the most elegant demonstrations that babbling is linguistic, not just motor play. The brain seeks the building blocks of the language it is exposed to - sounds for speech, hand shapes for sign - and practices them in rhythm ([Petitto et al., 2001](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2023976)).
