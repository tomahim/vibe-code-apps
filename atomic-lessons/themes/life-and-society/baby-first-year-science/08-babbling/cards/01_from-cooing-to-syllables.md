title: "From cooing to syllables"

After the cooing phase, vocal play expands: squeals, growls, raspberries and long vowel explorations fill the middle of the first year. Researchers call these protophones - vocal experiments that are not yet speech but exercise all its machinery.

Then, around six to nine months, a qualitative shift occurs: canonical babbling. The baby begins producing well-formed syllables - consonant-vowel units like ba, da, ma - and strings them together: bababa, dadada. The timing matters. Speech scientist Kimbrough Oller established that this transition, unlike earlier vocal play, follows a fairly tight schedule across typically developing infants.

Canonical babbling is the moment the vocal tract starts behaving like a speech instrument, with rhythmic jaw opening and closing sculpting sound into syllables. It sounds like talking because, mechanically, it finally is the talking motion - just without words yet.
