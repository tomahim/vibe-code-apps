Judging distance requires the brain to fuse the slightly different images from the two eyes, a skill called stereopsis. It emerges remarkably suddenly: preferential-looking studies find few infants show it before four months, most do by about four months, and nearly all by five to six months - one of the sharper transitions in perceptual development.

The classic demonstration is the visual cliff, devised by Eleanor Gibson and Richard Walk in 1960. Babies old enough to crawl were placed on a glass-topped platform with a checkered pattern that appeared to drop away. Most infants of crawling age hesitated or refused to cross the deep side, suggesting they perceived the drop - though the experiment tests babies who can already crawl, so it says more about the age of willingness than the birth of depth perception.

Depth vision feeds directly into the motor story of the last lessons: reaching, cruising and navigating stairs all depend on reading distance accurately.
