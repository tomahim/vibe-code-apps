Vision develops through what babies select. Even newborns show looking preferences, and by two to three months infants actively choose: they favor faces, moving objects, and patterns of moderate complexity - challenging enough to be interesting, simple enough to process. Too easy is boring; too complex gets ignored.

This selectivity is a built-in learning engine. By spending more time on stimuli they can almost handle, babies steer their own development, a pattern sometimes called the Goldilocks effect of attention. The world is sampled at the edge of current ability.

Caregivers plug directly into this engine. When a baby looks at something and the adult names or touches it - the serve-and-return pattern from lesson 7 - the baby's own attention sets the curriculum. Shared attention to the same object is one of the strongest known supports for later language learning.
