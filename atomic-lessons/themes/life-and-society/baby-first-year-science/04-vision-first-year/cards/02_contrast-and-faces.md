title: "High contrast and a hunger for faces"

Because newborn vision is blurry, bold contrasts carry best. Black-and-white edges, strong outlines and simple patterns are the easiest things for young infants to see, which is why high-contrast books and toys exist for this age.

One category wins above all: faces. In the 1960s, psychologist Robert Fantz showed that newborns look longer at patterned images than plain ones, and especially at face-like arrangements, launching the modern study of infant perception. Later work refined the picture: newborns prefer any top-heavy configuration resembling a face, and over the first months this general bias sharpens into genuine face expertise.

By a few months, babies distinguish familiar from unfamiliar faces, follow a caregiver's gaze direction, and begin reading expressions. Research tracking infants' attention across the first year shows faces occupying a growing share of looking, the foundation for the social learning that follows ([development of attention to faces](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2663531/)).
