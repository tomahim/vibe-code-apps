Looking is not the only instrument. The high-amplitude sucking technique gives babies a pacifier wired to a computer: when a sound plays, babies suck faster; as they habituate, sucking slows; a new sound that speeds sucking again has been detected as different. DeCasper and Fifer's mother-voice study from lesson 5 ran on exactly this instrument.

The conditioned head-turn works for older infants: a baby learns that a particular sound change is followed by an interesting visual reward, so turning the head toward the reward at the sound change reveals discrimination. Much of what we know about infant speech-sound discrimination across the first year came from this technique.

And beneath behavior lie physiological measures: heart-rate deceleration marks orienting to novelty, and cortisol in saliva tracks stress. Each proxy answers slightly different questions, and the strongest conclusions in the field come where several proxies agree.
