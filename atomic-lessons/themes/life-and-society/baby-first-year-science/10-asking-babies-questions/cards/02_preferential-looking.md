The founding tool was elegantly simple. In the late 1950s and 1960s, Robert Fantz placed babies in a looking chamber, showed them two images side by side, and had an observer record through a peephole which image the baby looked at longer. If looking times differ reliably, the baby must be telling the two apart - and must have a preference.

This single method established that newborns see patterns rather than blur alone, prefer complexity they can handle, and favor face-like arrangements. It also powered measurement: by showing gratings of finer and finer stripes against gray, Fantz could estimate acuity from the finest stripe a baby still preferred to look at - the origin of the 20/400 newborn estimate from lesson 4.

Preferential looking remains in daily use in infant labs worldwide, upgraded with eye-tracking cameras that measure gaze to the millisecond, but the logic is unchanged: what a baby chooses to look at is data.
