Direct-to-chip cooling puts a cold plate, a copper block with internal channels, directly on each hot chip. Liquid flows through the plates, absorbs the heat at the source, and carries it out through tubes to manifolds running down the rack, which connect the rack to the facility. The chips keep their heat sinks' job, but the heat now leaves the server in liquid, not air.

The engineering lives in the details: quick-disconnect couplings that let technicians remove a server without draining anything, leak detection and drip-less connectors, because liquid and electronics are uneasy neighbors, and coolant chemistry managed to prevent corrosion and biological growth. The liquid heat-capture fraction depends on which components have cold plates and on workload. Remaining heat from unplated components still leaves by air, so the data hall keeps its air systems, just downsized. It is a hybrid world, and knowing that split is key to sizing both halves.
