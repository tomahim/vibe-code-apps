The first objection every engineer raises to liquid cooling is the oldest fear in computing: water near electronics. The industry's answers are layered. Some systems use negative-pressure circulation so a breach tends to draw air in. Many others use positive-pressure loops with different leak controls; do not assume a cold-plate system is under vacuum. Quick-disconnect fittings are dry-break, sealing both sides as they separate. Leak detection cables snake through racks and under manifolds, alarming on the first drop.

Coolant choice adds another layer: heat-exchanger-based CDUs separate a controlled IT coolant loop from facility water. Other topologies exist, so coolant, materials and water quality must follow the specified design. The record so far supports the engineering: supercomputing centers have run liquid-cooled machines for decades, and the GB200 generation is industrializing the same practices for everyone. The fear is rational and the mitigation is systematic, which is exactly how this industry treats every risk from fire to grid failure: not by denying it, but by designing it down to a managed nonevent.
