Current, measured in amperes or amps (A), is the rate at which electric charge flows. In the water analogy, it is the volume of water moving through the pipe each second. In a simple DC example, a 10 kW load at 400 V draws 25 A. AC distribution needs an extra distinction: voltage and current may be out of phase, and three-phase circuits carry power on three conductors. At unity power factor, a balanced 300 MW, 138 kV three-phase feed draws about 1,255 A per line, not 300 MW divided by 138 kV.

Current matters for a practical reason: it is what heats wires. The power lost as heat in a conductor grows with the square of the current (P loss = I squared times R, the resistance). Double the current and you quadruple the heat. This single equation explains why grids transmit power at very high voltage: high voltage means low current for the same power, so far less energy is wasted warming up the cables. It also explains why the copper inside a data center, from busbars to cable whips, is sized with such care.
