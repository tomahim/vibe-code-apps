For decades, UPS batteries were valve-regulated lead-acid (VRLA): heavy, cheap, proven, and needy, with a service life of a few years and a dislike of heat. The industry is now shifting to lithium-ion, and SemiAnalysis notes the replacement is underway: lithium packs more energy into less space and weight, lasts longer, tolerates higher temperatures and needs less maintenance, which is why it dominates new builds.

The trade-off is fire safety. Lithium batteries store energy densely and can enter thermal runaway if damaged or poorly managed, so fire codes impose strict requirements: detection, suppression and often dedicated fire-resistant rooms. This constraint shapes architecture. Centralized UPS halls isolate the batteries from the data floor, while distributed designs like in-rack battery backup units put lithium next to servers and must invest in advanced suppression to satisfy the codes. Every battery decision in a data center is really three decisions: energy, safety and money.
