The transistor is the atom of modern computing: a microscopic switch with no moving parts, opened and closed by an electrical signal on its gate. When it conducts, we call the state 1; when it blocks, 0. Combine transistors and you can build logic gates, adders, memory cells and ultimately every operation a computer performs.

What makes the transistor revolutionary is its size and speed. Manufactured with photolithography on silicon wafers, modern transistors are measured in nanometers, and a single chip contains tens of billions of them, switching billions of times per second. Each switching event moves a tiny packet of charge and dissipates a tiny amount of heat. Multiply a tiny heat by tens of billions of switches flipping gigahertz-fast and you get the defining engineering fact of the data center era: computation converts electricity into heat, almost perfectly, watt for watt.
