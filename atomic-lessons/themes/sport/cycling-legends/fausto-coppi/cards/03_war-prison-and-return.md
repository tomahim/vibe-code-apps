The Second World War took Coppi to North Africa as an Italian soldier, where he was captured by the British in 1943 and spent time as a prisoner of war. When he came home in 1945 he was thin, weak and short of racing, but his class resurfaced almost immediately.

He won the Giro d'Italia again in 1947, seven years after his first, and resumed his place at the head of Italian cycling. The post-war years turned him into a national symbol: in a country rebuilding itself, his long solo attacks through the Alps and Dolomites gave crowds something transcendent to cheer. His rivalry with Gino Bartali, which split Italian fans into two camps, became the defining sporting drama of the era.
