Human reviewers can compare or rate model outputs. Their judgments provide a signal about which responses are preferred.

Imagine showing reviewers two answers to “Explain photosynthesis to a child.” One is short and accurate; the other is needlessly technical or wrong. Their comparison says which output better serves the request. Many such judgments can guide training toward preferred behavior. Reviewers can disagree, and their preferences depend on the instructions they were given, so the signal is not an objective measure of truth or universal values. It is nevertheless a practical way to teach qualities that ordinary next-token prediction does not directly optimize.
