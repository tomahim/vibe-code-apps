In November 1988 the internet was a small network of universities, research labs and military sites, roughly 60,000 computers connected through a few shared protocols. It was built by people who mostly knew each other, so machines often trusted one another by default. Nobody had designed it with an attacker in mind, and the World Wide Web did not exist yet.

That environment explains why the incident hit so hard. According to Spafford's analysis, weaknesses in the mail system and the "finger" directory service on Unix machines were exploited, and passwords could be guessed from a dictionary or from the account name. The worm did not need genius-level tricks. It needed a network where one weak machine could be used to reach the next one, and that is exactly what it found.
