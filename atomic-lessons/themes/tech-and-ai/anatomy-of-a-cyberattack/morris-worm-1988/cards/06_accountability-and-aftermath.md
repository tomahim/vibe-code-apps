The programmer was identified as Robert Tappan Morris, then a Cornell graduate student. According to the FBI, a friend of his told a reporter it was meant as a harmless experiment that spread because of a programming error. In 1990 he became the first person convicted under the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act of 1986, and he received three years of probation, 400 hours of community service and a fine of more than $10,000, not prison, as Cornell's alumni magazine reports.

The lasting change was institutional. The FBI notes that just days after the attack the first computer emergency response team was created in Pittsburgh, at the direction of the Department of Defense. Many countries later set up similar teams. The incident made patching, password hygiene and incident coordination into recognised professional work. Source: [FBI history page on the Morris Worm](https://www.fbi.gov/history/cases-and-criminals/morris-worm) and [Cornellians](https://alumni.cornell.edu/cornellians/morris-worm/).
