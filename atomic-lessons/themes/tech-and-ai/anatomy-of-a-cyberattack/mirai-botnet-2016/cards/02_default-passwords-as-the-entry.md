Mirai spread by scanning the internet for devices with an open Telnet service and then trying ten username and password pairs chosen from a built-in list of 62 common factory defaults, according to the USENIX paper. It did not need a clever exploit. When a login worked, the device was infected and began scanning for more victims.

The scale came from the sheer number of poorly secured devices. Many devices were left with the password the factory chose, and owners rarely watched them.

This is why the case is often used to explain defaults. A password that is the same on thousands of devices is effectively public, and a device that can be reached from the internet with a login prompt invites constant automated attempts from anywhere in the world.
