Once inside a company, NotPetya moved quickly. According to Wired, it combined the EternalBlue exploit, also used by WannaCry, with password-stealing code that reused credentials to reach other machines. This meant that a network could be vulnerable even if most machines had been patched, as long as credentials were exposed.

The malware then encrypted the computers' master boot record and files. The ransom note asked for $300 in Bitcoin and displayed a single email address. Analysts concluded that it was designed to destroy data, not to restore it, which is why it is described as a wiper in disguise.

Defenders can learn two lessons here. Patching closes one route, but credential hygiene closes another, so administrator accounts should be limited and not reused across machines. Network segmentation then limits how far a single infected machine can reach.
