# A question on the shore

On a breezy morning, fictional young explorer Kit met fossil collector Mary Anning. The beach near Lyme Regis was scattered with pebbles. Some held shapes that looked like secrets. Mary showed Kit a coiled ammonite.

"A shell can be a clue," Kit thought. The creature was gone, yet part of its story remained in stone. They stayed on a safe route, well away from unstable cliffs.

> Always explore coasts with an adult and follow local safety advice.

# A sea inside a stone

Kit traced the ammonite's spiral with a finger. Long ago, animals with shells like these had lived in the sea. Fossils are preserved remains or traces of ancient life. They help us ask what a place was like before we were here.

Mary had learned to look carefully. An ordinary-looking rock could hold evidence. Kit decided that noticing small things was a kind of superpower.

# Patient eyes

Back at a table, Mary studied a fossil and made a careful sketch. Finding something was only part of the work. Cleaning, observing and comparing helped her understand it. Kit watched her draw one small detail at a time.

Mary Anning was a real fossil collector whose discoveries helped scientists study ancient life. Her work was important even when she did not receive the recognition she deserved.

> A good question starts with a careful look.

# The vanished swimmers

Kit imagined the sea as it had been long before. An ichthyosaur glided through the water. Its body looked a little like a dolphin's, but it was a marine reptile, not a dolphin and not a dinosaur.

Fossils give us clues to these vanished worlds. Pictures of ancient life include interpretation: no person stood there with a camera. Kit liked that science could hold both evidence and new questions.

> The model is a playful fossil spiral, not a reconstruction of an animal.

# Keep the question

As the tide shifted, Kit looked back at the shore. A day earlier it had been a beach full of stones. Now it felt like a library with very old pages.

Kit and this meeting are invented. Mary Anning was born in Lyme Regis in 1799. She found and studied remarkable fossils, including marine reptiles. Her story reminds us that discovery belongs to curious people who observe, learn and keep asking.

> If a stone could tell a story, what would you ask it?
