# The windiest beach in America

Sam loved the huge sandy dunes near his home in Kitty Hawk, North Carolina. In the autumn of 1903, two brothers from Ohio arrived with crates full of wooden struts and cloth. They were building a flying machine.

Wilbur and Orville Wright were bicycle makers, not famous scientists. They had chosen Sam's beach for its soft sand and steady wind. Sam decided he would help them, or at least watch very carefully.

# Lessons from birds

Long before engines, the brothers watched birds twist their wingtips to turn. Back in their bicycle workshop, they built gliders and tested hundreds of little wing shapes inside a homemade wind tunnel, a wooden box with a fan.

Year after year they returned to the dunes, learning to balance and steer in the air. With more than a thousand practice glides from the biggest dune, they became skilled pilots before their machine even had an engine.

> A wind tunnel lets inventors test wings safely on the ground.

# A coin toss in the cold

At last, in December 1903, the Flyer was ready: two cloth wings, one above the other, and two propellers turned by a small engine. On December 14, the brothers tossed a coin. Wilbur won the first try.

But the Flyer climbed too steeply, stalled and slid into the sand. Repairs took three days while icy wind howled through the camp. Sam brought the brothers hot coffee and wondered if winter would win.

> To stall means a wing loses its lift and the machine stops flying.
---page---
# Twelve seconds

On the freezing morning of December 17, five helpers from the lifesaving station came to watch. At half past ten, Orville lay flat on the lower wing, the engine sputtered, and the Flyer rattled down its wooden rail.

Then it rose! Into a fierce wind, Orville flew just above the sand for twelve seconds, about the length of three buses, before touching down. Sam shouted so loudly that the gulls scattered. It was the first powered, controlled airplane flight in history.

> Those 120 feet were shorter than the wings of a modern jumbo jet.
---page---
# The telegram

The brothers flew three more times that day, taking turns. On the last flight, Wilbur stayed up for fifty-nine seconds and crossed 852 feet of sand before a gust cracked a skid. Nobody minded. They had done it.

That evening, a telegraph operator tapped the news to the brothers' family in Dayton, Ohio: success, four flights, home by Christmas. Sam is invented, but the flight is real. Within a lifetime, airplanes would carry people across oceans and toward the stars.

> True history, imagined neighbor. What would you invent with a workshop and a windy hill?
