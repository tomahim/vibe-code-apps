The regular division of the plane

A tessellation is a covering of the plane by shapes that repeat without gaps or overlaps. Squares, equilateral triangles and regular hexagons do it on their own - they are the three regular tilings, and each one is the starting point of the Studio tab.

M.C. Escher (1898-1972), a Dutch graphic artist, turned that dry fact into an art form. Struck by the interlocking patterns of the Alhambra palace in Spain, which he visited in 1922 and again in 1936, he spent decades on what he called the "regular division of the plane". His method was exactly the one you just used: take a tile that already tiles, deform one edge, and let a symmetry rule copy the deformation onto the matching edge so the tile still closes. Then he looked at the silhouette until a lizard, a bird or a fish looked back - and drew it in.

Beneath the craft sits a clean piece of mathematics: there are exactly seventeen ways a pattern can repeat across the plane, the seventeen wallpaper groups. This studio plays with two of the simplest - repetition by translation alone (called p1) and by half-turns (p2). Escher worked through all seventeen in his notebooks.
